# Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia

## Source identity and access

- Wheel source ID: `black-green-mesopotamia`
- Registered authors: Jeremy Black and Anthony Green (illustrations by Tessa
  Rickards)
- Registered date: 1992 (British Museum Press; the inspected scan is a later
  printing of the illustrated dictionary)
- Access level: `excerpt`

The relevant dictionary entries were inspected in the public Internet Archive
scan of the illustrated dictionary
(`gods-demons-and-symbols-of-ancient-mesopotamia-an-illustrated-dictionary`).
The entries *Anuna (Anunnakkū)* (p. 34), *Igigū* (p. 103), and the underworld
discussion on p. 179 were read directly. The monograph was not read in full,
so the access level is `excerpt` rather than `full_text`.

## Source type and purpose

A scholarly illustrated dictionary of Mesopotamian religion aimed at a general
and student readership. The public Anunnaki comparison cites it for the
Anunnaki/Igigi collectives.

## Reported content

The inspected entries report (quoted from the scan):

- *Anuna (Anunnakkū)*, p. 34: the term "possibly means 'princely offspring',
  is used in earlier, especially Sumerian, texts as a general word for the
  gods, in particular the early gods who were born first and were not
  differentiated with individual names." Anu "is described as king of the
  Anunnakku," and in the Epic of Creation "the multitude of gods are called the
  'Anunnakki of heaven and earth'." From Middle Babylonian times, following the
  use of *Igigi* for the gods of heaven, "the term Anunnakkū came to be used
  more for the gods of earth (Ki) and underworld."
- *Igigū*, p. 103: the term was "introduced in the Old Babylonian Period as a
  name for the (ten) 'great gods'," and "from Middle Babylonian times on … is
  generally used to refer to the gods of heaven collectively, just as the term
  Anunnakki (Anuna) was later used to refer to the gods of the underworld."
- p. 179: "in later Babylonian texts 600 Anunnakkū (Anuna) gods are assigned to
  the underworld," reflecting "the development of a more precise picture of the
  nether regions."

## Basis or method

A reference dictionary synthesizing Assyriological scholarship. The entries
above are the authors' summary of the collectives' shifting sense across
Sumerian, Old Babylonian, Middle Babylonian, and later periods; they cite the
Epic of Creation for the specific counts.

## Limitations and criticism

- This is a dictionary summary, not a primary text or a monographic study of
  either collective; period-by-period attestations must ultimately be checked
  against the primary corpus.
- The entries establish that the Anunnaki/Igigi reference is *unstable* across
  periods. They document a moving convention, which limits any claim that
  "Anunnaki" names one constant group.
- Only three entries were inspected; other relevant entries (e.g. individual
  gods named in the comparison) were not.

## Wheel interpretation

**Interpretation:** the source functions as scholarly context for claim
`woh-claim-0002`, and on inspection it *constrains* rather than supports the
identity claim. Because the Anunnaki referent migrates from "undifferentiated
early gods" to "gods of the underworld," an identity claim must specify which
period's Anunnaki it means before it can be assessed. The source supplies no
lexical or textual bridge between the Mesopotamian collectives and the Hebrew
`Elohim`; that bridge is added by the framework.
