# The East Face of Helicon

## Source identity and access

Wheel source ID
`the-east-face-of-helicon-west-asiatic-elements-in-greek-poetry-and-myth`.
M. L. West, *The East Face of Helicon: West Asiatic Elements in Greek Poetry
and Myth* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1997). The workspace holds a complete
research copy. The chapters used by the Greek article sequence were read in
full from a text-bearing scan; every short quotation retained for public use
was then checked against a rendered page image.

The book remains in copyright. Access is for research and verification only;
the file must not be redistributed. Extracted text is a finding aid, not a
quotation source, because OCR and text layers can corrupt transliterated
Akkadian, Ugaritic, Hittite, and Greek.

## Source type and purpose

Comparative philology and literary history. West asks how extensively archaic
Greek poetry was shaped by the older literatures of Mesopotamia, Anatolia,
Syria-Phoenicia, and related eastern Mediterranean contact zones. His method
is cumulative: individual comparisons carry unequal weight, and the argument
depends on the density and variety of correspondences across mythic plot,
divine typology, narrative procedure, formula, diction, and historically
plausible contact.

## Reported content

The passages this repository currently relies on are:

- Preface vii–ix and chapter 1, pp. 59–60 — the cumulative method; differences
  between texts remain real, not disproofs, and most comparisons are not claims
  that a Greek poet consulted a specific written source directly.
- Chapter 4, pp. 168–174 and 190–191 — comparisons in verse form, poem
  openings, procedural narrative, speeches, assemblies, messenger sequences,
  and similes. West does not derive the Greek hexameter from an eastern metre,
  and he identifies the invocation of the Muses as characteristically Greek.
- Chapter 5, pp. 220–226 — formulaic epithets and recurring idiom. Some
  correspondences are independently plausible; the force lies in a graded
  pattern, not in counting every shared phrase as a loan.
- Chapter 6, pp. 276–304 and 332 — the Ouranos–Kronos–Zeus succession beside
  the Anu–Kumarbi–Teshub succession; related Babylonian material; and the
  wider storm-god combat family. West treats the Typhoeus episode as a
  separable combat narrative rather than forcing every conflict into one
  inherited plot.
- Chapter 8, pp. 402–417 and 437 — the *Odyssey* wanderings in relation to
  the Standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh* epic: proems, island women, the
  Phaeacian threshold, descent to the dead, wakefulness, and the sacred
  cattle. West argues for a literary relationship in the wanderings strand
  while explicitly rejecting identity between Odysseus and Gilgamesh; he
  also judges the *Odyssey* overall less densely eastern than the *Iliad*.
- Chapter 12, pp. 586–630 — possible periods, routes, and agents of
  transmission: Mycenaean contact, Cyprus after the palace collapse, the
  Levantine and Anatolian corridor, Crete, Pithekoussai, traders, craftsmen,
  interpreters, mercenaries, migrants, and bilingual singers. The contact
  field is historical; reconstruction of a particular story's journey is
  usually probabilistic.

## Basis or method

West combines close textual comparison with chronology and contact history.
The sequence matters. A proposed dependence becomes stronger when the eastern
text is demonstrably older, the correspondence is unusually articulated,
several related features cluster together, and a route of contact is
available. It becomes weaker when the motif is generic, the phrase could
easily arise twice, chronology is uncertain, or the proposed route is only a
scenario. This record preserves those grades rather than flattening the book
into a catalogue of resemblances.

## Limitations and criticism

- The work is programmatically maximal. A responsible derivative should not
  convert the cumulative thesis into equal certainty for every comparison.
- The source demonstrates influence and dependence, not the proportion of all
  Greek tradition that was imported. Indo-European inheritance, Aegean
  survivals, and Greek recomposition remain indispensable.
- Most exact transmission paths cannot be recovered. Bilingual immigrant
  singers are a plausible mechanism in West's reconstruction, not documented
  biographies of the poets who made the surviving works.
- The 1997 synthesis does not substitute for a systematic review of later
  scholarship. The core claim therefore remains `scoped`.
- Literary transmission can explain shared narrative material without a
  shared historical referent. West's argument supplies no evidence for the
  Wheel's Elohimian interpretation.

## Wheel interpretation

Claim `woh-claim-0005` records West's comparative and transmission case at
`direct` strength. Claims `woh-claim-0004` and `woh-claim-0007` begin only
after that case has been stated: they ask whether transmitted Greek and Near
Eastern structures also preserve transformed memory of a shared referent.
That second move is a `speculative` or `framework` interpretation. The source
neither makes nor requires it, and the transmission-suffices alternative
remains fully live.
