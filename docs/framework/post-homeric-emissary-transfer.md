# Post-Homeric emissary transfer to Persia and Greece

## Exact proposition

According to the canon, the creators placed emissaries ("angels") among the
Persians and the Greeks to accomplish technology transfer during the era of
those powers' domination of Israel, as a punitive inversion of providence.
That era runs from the sixth to the fourth century BCE and therefore
post-dates the crystallisation of the Homeric poems: the emissary episode
explains no Homeric or Hesiodic content.

This is an `interpretation` with framework relation `derived` and public
label `framework`. The canonical report is explicit; the chronological
corollary — the part that does the work — is derived from it.

## The canonical basis

`TBWTT-3:213` reads Israel's domination by Persia and Greece as deliberate:
the creators, "to punish it for its lack of faith, placed men of theirs,
'angels', among these peoples, in order to have them accomplish technical
progress." `3:214–215` names Michael as head of the delegation to Persia,
quoting Daniel ("Michael … came … there, beside the kings of Persia" —
Daniel 10:13, 10:20, where Greece's "prince" also appears).

## Why the chronological boundary is the point

The domination era the canon reads — Achaemenid Persia over Judea from 539
BCE, the Hellenistic kingdoms after Alexander — begins roughly two centuries
after the Homeric and Hesiodic poems crystallised. So there are **two
transfers into Greece, centuries apart, with different mechanisms**: the
documented literary transmission of the archaic period (`woh-claim-0005`,
human routes, poets and traders), and the canonical emissary engagement of
the classical era (this record). Blurring them would hand the framework an
explanation for Homer that its own chronology forbids. The boundary is
written into this record's scope precisely so no future artifact can use the
emissaries to explain archaic material.

## Alternatives and challenges

The mainstream readings of Daniel 10 are recorded as alternatives: patron
angels of the nations within Second Temple angelology; the theology of
foreign domination; and the critical observation that the canon retrofits a
technology-transfer frame onto a text usually dated, in its final form, to
the second century BCE — after the events it frames. That dating point cuts
at the canon's reading and is preserved at full strength.
