# Sendy and the Messages — resemblance, difference, dependence

## Exact proposition

The inspected French witnesses of Jean Sendy's books and the first Raëlian Message share a substantial cluster of technological biblical reinterpretations, including forbidden knowledge as books, the serpent's punishment as confinement to Earth, and Noah's preservation of biological material.
They nevertheless give that material different biological histories, narrative agents, sources of authority, and expectations of renewed contact.
These observations make selective literary adaptation a serious hypothesis; they establish neither direct dependence nor composition independently of Sendy.
Differences cannot establish independence, and resemblance cannot establish copying without further source-critical work.

An `interpretation`, `comparative`, `inferred`; evidence `scoped` and `contested`; lifecycle **draft; founder authorized article drafting after reviewing the proposed framing**.
This record examines the texts and leaves the truth of extraterrestrial contact unresolved.

## Scope and controls

The controlling comparison is the French Sendy material supplied in `unversioned/sendy/` against the French base text of *Le Livre qui dit la vérité* in the project digitization.
The latter represents the 2005 collected edition.
The 1974 first printing remains to be inspected.
Sendy's EPUBs are later digital witnesses of earlier works, and the *Ces dieux* file identifies a 1979 reissue.
Passages in later editions require collation before they can be attributed to the first edition.
No claim of verbatim plagiarism or numerical probability is authorized by this record.

The argument rests on five observations:

1. Several particular biblical reinterpretations recur together alongside the shared premise of ancient visitors.
2. Sendy explicitly accepts natural evolution while speculating about subsequent restoration, breeding, and genetic intervention; the first Message redescribes the succession of living forms as the creators' developing techniques.
   Sendy also criticizes evolutionary explanations, and his position differs from contemporary evolutionary biology.
3. In *Ces dieux*, the original visitors have left, and their return and direct advice to later prophets are rejected as highly implausible; the Messages make continuing contact central.
   Chapter 35 explicitly describes the original visitors as deceased and proposes living descendants elsewhere. This distinguishes the original personnel from the survival of their lineage; it does not support extinction of all Elohim.
4. Sendy's medievalism, preserved Tradition, and speculative reconstruction differ from the Messages' encounter testimony, personalized commission, and embassy programme.
   Some philosophical and political features of the Messages also have precedents in Sendy.
5. Direct borrowing, mediated influence, and independent recombination of common materials remain distinct explanations.
   An author can transform borrowed material into a work with a different purpose.

## Strongest case for dependence

The combined readings of forbidden fruit as books and the serpent’s punishment as confinement to Earth warrant investigation as possible evidence of borrowing.
Sendy also makes an apparent contemporary accusation against an unnamed young lecturer using the Elohim name; Bigliardi identifies the target as Raël.
An explanation based on coincidence must account for these particular readings.

The case remains limited by three gaps: evidence that Raël encountered Sendy before composition, a comparison of the French first editions, and a survey of comparable contemporary literature.
Biblical quotations and their sequence are inherited from a common source and cannot be counted as independent matches.

## Strongest case for independence or indirect influence

Common biblical material, scientific popularization, esoteric traditions, and other ancient-astronaut literature provide plausible shared resources.
Sendy cites earlier writers himself.
The sources may therefore share inherited readings, or one may have reached the other through intermediaries.
Testing this explanation requires tracing the particular shared readings through earlier sources.

The Messages attribute their account to extraterrestrial speakers.
This literary comparison leaves that attribution unresolved.
The record does not authorize psychological judgments about either author or conclusions about the sincerity of adherents or former adherents.

## Revision 0.2.0 (2026-10-14): extent of the overlap and the post-Genesis gap

Source review against the full French texts found the v0.1.0 comparison understated several Genesis parallels: *Ces dieux* ch. 2 describes life's record as evolving "exactly as it would in a laboratory" of trial and error, including monstrous forms, while suspending judgment on its cause; ch. 9 restores plants, then herbivores, then carnivores for ecological balance; ch. 24 has celestial "semence" producing "demi-dieux" and an experimental population nicknamed « le peuple élu »; chs. 30–31 and 34 make the Flood a deliberate destruction decided by the Celestials; *La lune* ch. 9 floats Iahvé as the title of the Elohim's elected president; *L'ère* ch. 17 and *La lune* date the arrival around −22,000, the Message's figure (5:4) for the creators' decision.

A full inventory of the first Message's commentary (226 items; `docs/research/sendy-inventory/`) was classified against the five pre-1974 Sendy books. Genesis (Message ch. 2): 44 items, none absent. Israel, prophets, Jesus (chs. 3–4): 147 items, 105 absent, 29 mentioned without the Message's kind of reading, 13 similar or identical. Sendy's books exclude return, radio contact with prophets, and visitors at the burning bush, and *L'ère* denies Ezekiel saw cosmonauts.

Revised adjudication: selective adaptation is a serious hypothesis for the Genesis chapter; it cannot account for the book as a whole without a further, unidentified body of sources. Absence from Sendy is not originality relative to the unsurveyed 1960s literature, and the result does not establish independent composition. The canon's own account of the commentary's origin may be reported in framework voice, labelled as such.
