# The Elohim plural and the canonical gloss

## Exact proposition

*Elohim* is grammatically plural and the biblical text preserves plural
usage around it; the corpus reads that plurality referentially — many
individual Elohim — and adopts the canon's gloss "those who came from the
sky," recording explicitly that the gloss is a canonical rendering and not
the consensus lexical derivation.

This is an `interpretation` with framework relation `derived` and public
label `framework`.

## Provenance

Distilled at founder direction from the most-referenced entry in the corpus
(`wiki/elohim.md`, 61 in-corpus links) and `wiki/plurality-of-gods.md`. The
word this record disciplines appears on effectively every framework page,

per-page judgment.

## Three layers, kept distinct

1. **Philology (direct, mainstream):** the plural morphology; the plural
   cohortative of Genesis 1:26 and the "as one of us" of 3:22; *benei
   ha-elohim*; the Psalm 82 assembly. None of this is contested; none of it
   alone yields the framework reading.
2. **Reference (framework):** the corpus resolves the plural referentially,
   against the plural-of-majesty and heavenly-court readings, which are
   preserved as alternatives at full strength.
3. **The gloss (canonical, labelled):** "those who came from the sky" is
   the canon's rendering. Per the
   [epistemic principles](../foundations/epistemic-principles.md), a
   canonical gloss must be labelled as such when it is not the consensus
   lexical derivation — and it is not: the consensus derives *Elohim*
   through *Eloah*/*El*. Any public prose presenting the gloss as an
   etymology misexpresses this record.

The invalid transition the claim model names — morphological form to full
syntactic meaning without contextual analysis — is this record's central
discipline: the plural forms are the *occasion* for the framework reading,
never its proof.

## Alternatives and challenges

Plural of majesty; the heavenly-court reading (the standard scholarly
resolution of the council texts); the diachronic account (fossils of edited
polytheism — Smith); and the etymological objection to the gloss. The
diachronic account is the strongest: it explains every plural trace as
development, and the corpus's referential reading currently adds a referent
without adding a prediction.
