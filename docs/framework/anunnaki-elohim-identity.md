# Anunnaki–Elohim identity

## Exact proposition

Wheel of Heaven proposes that the Mesopotamian Anunnaki and the Elohim refer to
the same underlying civilization, remembered through different languages and
cultural records.

This is a primary `interpretation` with framework relation `comparative` and
public label `framework`.

## Dependency

The proposition depends on the
[Elohim-civilization hypothesis](elohim-civilization-hypothesis.md). If claim
`woh-claim-0001` is revised or retired, this claim must be reassessed.

The dependency is one-way: the foundational hypothesis does not require the
Anunnaki identity claim.

## What is being identified

The current proposition operates at collective level. It does not establish
that:

- every Mesopotamian divine name maps to a particular Eloha;
- every use of `Anunnaki`, `Igigi`, `Elohim`, `El`, or another collective has a
  stable roster across texts and periods;
- similar flood, creation, council, or dissenter motifs name the same event;
- popular etymologies are valid;
- Zecharia Sitchin's Nibiru, orbit, gold-mining, or genetic chronology follows
  from the identity claim.

## Current comparative basis

The existing public argument compares recurring structures including plural or
collective makers, divine councils, the fashioning of humanity, labor assigned
to humans, conflict among divine actors, flood decisions, and a warning or
preservation figure.

The inspected Wheel translation of *Atra-ḫasīs* records at
`ATRA-WOH-I:195` that humankind is to bear the labor of the gods. This is a
relevant textual observation for comparison. It does not name the Elohim or
establish collective identity.

Scholarly contextual sources such as Black and Green are cited by the public
article for the changing sense of the Anunnaki and Igigi. The relevant
dictionary entries have now been inspected in the public scan (*Anuna*, p. 34;
*Igigū*, p. 103; underworld count, p. 179), so the source note is `excerpt`
rather than `metadata_only`. On inspection the entries document a *shifting*
referent — the Anunnaki move from undifferentiated early gods to gods of the
underworld across periods — which constrains rather than supports a
constant-roster identity claim. See the
[source note](../../source-notes/black-green-mesopotamia.md).

## Required inferential steps

For the identity claim to advance beyond a framework interpretation, review
would need to address:

1. the exact Mesopotamian passages and changing uses of the relevant
   collectives;
2. the exact biblical passages and changing uses of `Elohim`;
3. textual dates, genres, transmission, and possible historical dependence;
4. shared features and important mismatches;
5. whether common inheritance, borrowing, convergent narrative function, or
   coincidence explains the comparison more economically;
6. criteria capable of distinguishing typological parallel from referential
   identity.

## Alternatives and challenges

The strongest alternative families currently include:

- the terms refer to different divine collectives within distinct traditions;
- similarities arise through ancient Near Eastern literary inheritance or
  borrowing without literal shared beings;
- similarities are typological products of creation and flood narrative
  functions;
- later interpreters selected congenial parallels from internally diverse
  corpora;
- the Raëlian framework supplies the identity mapping rather than the ancient
  texts themselves.

These alternatives may coexist in part. For example, literary dependence can
explain shared narrative structures without deciding the historical reality of
either tradition's reported beings.

## Revision triggers

Revise, split, or retire this specification when:

- the collective-level proposition proves too broad across periods and texts;
- source-language review materially changes the relevant observations;
- a comparison matrix supports only narrower motif or episode relationships;
- the dependency on claim `woh-claim-0001` changes;
- public prose adds individual mappings or Sitchin-derived mechanisms not
  present here.
