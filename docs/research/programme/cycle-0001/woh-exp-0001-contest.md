## The strongest objection

The expectation's title and its lexicon protocol are about what Hebrew words mean. Outside the excluded Elohim gloss, the book makes no such statement that could be tested. None of the five `unsupported` rows says "the Hebrew word X means Y". Row by row:

- **A1, "jour" = about 2,000 years (TBWTT-2:10).** The verb is "correspond à"; the speaker says what the day of the narrative stands for, not what יוֹם means. It is a decoding gloss of the List B kind, which the finding itself rules to be "identifications of a referent, not claims about Hebrew" and keeps out of the totals. Either A1 leaves the count, or List B enters it. The finding cannot have both.
- **A3b, "et non pas sur les eaux" (TBWTT-2:60).** A statement of what the text says, so within the derivation ("statements about the wording of the text"). Read at clause level it is accurate: the clause is וַתָּרָם מֵעַל הָאָרֶץ and has no water in it. Read at verse level it is false: the same verse makes the waters lift the ark, and the next has it go on the face of the waters. The book's own quotation at 2:59 marks two omissions with "(...)", the second exactly where the Hebrew has the waters. This is selective quotation. Whether selective quotation is "the Hebrew does not read so" is a convention; it also exists only because the Locator split one sentence in two.
- **A4, "il est dit plus loin qu'elle dura «presque un jour entier»" (TBWTT-3:71).** A direct report of what the text says, and the Hebrew does not say it: the subject of the clause with כְּיוֹם תָּמִים is the sun, not the war. A defender will say the speaker is paraphrasing inside his own reading (the standing sun is the short war). That is so, but the sentence is offered as the textual ground of the reading ("puisqu'il est dit"), and as a report of the wording it is wrong. This row stands.
- **A5, "sains" for "saints" (TBWTT-3:192).** The speaker does not say the Hebrew word means "healthy". He says the writers should have written something else, and so concedes that the text says "holy". That is a claim about what lies behind the text, like A6 and A2, and by the finding's own practice `not_checkable`. What the row does show is of a different kind and is not softened by reclassifying it: the correction works only in French, where the two words sound alike; nothing in קֹדֶשׁ answers to it. That is evidence about the language in which the remark was composed. A defender will answer that the speaker was addressing a Frenchman in French. The observation belongs under adjacent evidence for the source-critical alternative, not in a lexicon count.
- **A7, Matt 5:3 (TBWTT-4:24).** The only row in which the speaker sets an "original sense" against a translation, and it fails against the only original extant. But the text is Greek, no Hebrew lexicon applies, and no Greek lexicon was consulted. The expectation's statement says "Hebrew or biblical word"; its question says "the original (Hebrew) text"; its instrument is a Hebrew lexicon. The record does not settle whether this row is in scope.

On a strict count, then: one row firmly `unsupported` (A4); one that depends on reading "il est dit" at verse level (A3b); one that depends on admitting Greek (A7); two that are mis-scoped or inconsistently classed (A1, A5). One suffices for the registered miss criterion. The assessment follows from the criteria; the figure "5 of 10" does not survive.

Was the expectation fairly derived? Partly. The derivation moves from "the speaker is what the claim says" to "his statements about the wording should be sound". That step needs two premises the record does not list under `auxiliary_assumptions`: that the book transmits the speaker's words exactly, and that the speaker is doing philology on the transmitted text. The book says otherwise about its own method: the speaker announces that he will "traduire" the important parts (TBWTT-2:3), meaning explain them, and holds that the text is "un peu déformé" (2:2) and in places "des plus déformés" (3:69). A reader of the book would have predicted many glosses that no lexicon bears out, because that is the book's programme. If List B counts, the miss was certain before anyone looked and shows a careless derivation. If List B does not count, the miss rests on A4 and its neighbours, was not certain, and is a fair result.

Was the search adequate? For a miss, yes. Chapters 1 and 4-7 were searched by term, not read; further statements could add to the count but cannot remove A4. One gap cuts the other way: A8 (RAËL "veut dire littéralement «lumière de Dieu»") is the nearest thing in the book to a lexical statement, the speaker says his language is close to ancient Hebrew (7:35), and the Locator records that no search was made in BDB for an element meaning "light". The row is untested, not shown to be untestable. Only one lexicon and no commentary were consulted, so `contested` could not arise; this could matter at most for A1, where "day" as an indefinite period is a known reading, though a fixed 2,000 years is not.

## What the result does and does not show

It shows that in the first book the speaker at least once reports the wording of a verse wrongly (Josh 10:13) and at least once quotes selectively in a way the verse does not bear (Gen 7:17). In both places the Hebrew agrees with the French Bible the book quotes, so the error is not one of Hebrew. It is a reader's error against the page in front of him. The source-critical alternative predicted error "where the book goes beyond what its French Bible prints"; what was found is error where the book departs from what its French Bible prints. That fits the alternative's general picture of an author reading selectively, but it is not the specific thing the rival note predicted, a false statement about Hebrew.

It does not show that the book's statements about Hebrew words are lexically unsupported, because, the Elohim gloss apart, the book makes almost none. The one clear lexical statement in the book was excluded at registration as already recorded (woh-claim-0009), and it is the one already known not to be the consensus derivation. With that statement removed, the test had little left to bite on.

Read with woh-exp-0006 (one family, one result): neither book, outside the Elohim gloss, offers a testable statement of what a Hebrew word means. Where the first book comments on wording, it misstates it once or twice. Where the second book only quotes or alludes, the quotations are as accurate as the French Bible they come from. The miss here and the hit there are not opposed outcomes of one test. They are produced by two inclusion conventions: this finding left plain quotations out of List A ("Plain restatements of a quoted verse were not listed"), and the 0006 finding built its whole count from plain quotations and allusions. Under one convention applied to both, the family reads either "miss, and a hit that means nothing" or "little to test in either book".

The result bears on the report as a text and on the identification's hooks only, as the derivation says. It says nothing about events.

## What the ruling turns on

1. Whether "what the original text says" covers any report of a verse's wording (then A4 stands and the miss is fair), or only statements that invoke the original or the Hebrew (then nothing checkable remains and the registered outcome is the partial case).
2. Whether decoding glosses count as statements of word meaning. If they do, the miss was certain and the expectation was badly formed. If they do not, A1 leaves the count.
3. Whether a miss carried by one or two misreported verses, neither of them an error about Hebrew, should be recorded as a miss of this expectation as titled, or in a narrower form that names what was actually found.
4. Whether the two unstated premises (exact transmission; a speaker doing philology on the transmitted text) are auxiliary assumptions, so that the miss counts against them and not against the identification.
