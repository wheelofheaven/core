## Answer

**What the paper claims.** The paper claims that the standard genetic code (the codon-to-amino-acid mapping) contains an ensemble of arithmetical patterns (equalities of nucleon sums of amino acids, written with distinctive decimal notation, multiples of 037) and "ideographical" symmetry patterns, that these are statistically significant, and that they are artificial in origin ("an intelligent signature"), consistent with intentional seeding of terrestrial life. So the paper does claim artificial patterning.

**Stated statistical support.** The abstract (preprint PDF version) states the null hypothesis of chance plus presumable evolutionary pathways "is rejected with P-value < 10–13". Appendix B derives this as a product of three computer-estimated probabilities from samples of one billion random codes generated under three imposed "evolutionary pathway" conditions: p1 = 1.5×10–8 (nucleon equalities), p2 = 9.4×10–5 (ideogram symmetries), p3 = 1.7×10–2 (transformations in Rumer's bisection), product 2.4×10–14. The authors themselves qualify the test as "greatly simplified" and the P-value as "a rough approximation".

**Later literature.** Among the works screened I found:
- no peer-reviewed publication that argues the patterns are artefacts of chance, of selection among arrangements, or of known biochemistry;
- no peer-reviewed analysis, free of shared authorship, that sets out to test and confirm the patterns;
- one peer-reviewed extension by the same two authors (Makukov and shCherbak 2018, Int. J. Astrobiology);
- one independent-author book chapter in an edited almanac (Panov and Filatov 2024), whose peer-review status I could not establish, which re-presents and adds to the numerical "signatures", says not all of the original constructions are "flawless", and argues that the probability of accidental occurrence cannot be quantified at all;
- peer-reviewed works by other authors that use or build on shCherbak's nucleon-number/proline scheme or mention the paper as background without testing it (Négadi 2023, 2024; Filatov 2022, 2026; Kocharyan 2026; Pappolla 2026, which tests a different object, human ultraconserved DNA).

**Classification: `unrebutted_unreplicated`.**

Caveats on this classification, which a reader should weigh:
1. Google Scholar, which normally lists more citers than Semantic Scholar or OpenCitations, could not be accessed. The classification rests on 32 indexed citers plus web search; a peer-reviewed rebuttal or confirmation outside that set is not excluded.
2. Panov and Filatov 2024 is the closest thing to an independent analysis. If it were counted as peer-reviewed, it still would not straightforwardly fit either other category: it accepts a subset of the patterns as real ("not objectionable"), reports one prediction of the artificiality hypothesis as "confirmed", but rejects the kind of probability estimate the original paper relies on and gives "No definite answer". ### Target paper (arXiv:1303.6739 PDF, authors' version)

Abstract, p. 1:
> "A statistically strong intelligent-like "signal" in the genetic code is then a testable consequence of such scenario. Here we show that the terrestrial code displays a thorough precision-type orderliness matching the criteria to be considered an informational signal. Simple arrangements of the code reveal an ensemble of arithmetical and ideographical patterns of the same symbolic language. Accurate and systematic, these underlying patterns appear as a product of precision logic and nontrivial computing rather than of stochastic processes (the null hypothesis that they are due to chance coupled with presumable evolutionary pathways is rejected with P-value < 10–13). The patterns are profound to the extent that the code mapping itself is uniquely deduced from their algebraic representation. The signal displays readily recognizable hallmarks of artificiality, among which are the symbol of zero, the privileged decimal syntax and semantical symmetries. Besides, extraction of the signal involves logically straightforward but abstract operations, making the patterns essentially irreducible to any natural origin."

## Adjacent observations

- The paper reports that it passed peer review at Icarus with three reviewers (Acknowledgments), and gives its history as submitted 26 June 2012, revised 3 October 2012 and 31 January 2013, accepted 12 February 2013.
- The authors' own framing of the hypothesis under test is directed panspermia / "seeded intentionally"; in the Pharyngula comment thread a reply attributed to an author says "we have no any relation to Intelligent Design, we just test the hypothesis of Crick and Orgel."
- The paper defines what would defeat the charge of data fishing in its own terms (Appendix B, p. 11): "the pattern ensemble employs informational capacity of the code entirely, showing that it represents a feature inherent to the code itself, rather than an artifact of data fishing." Panov and Filatov's Sect. 9.2 argument (the "search at corners" problem) addresses the same issue and reaches the opposite view about whether a probability can be assigned.
- The 2018 follow-up states that its analysis code "is available at https://sourceforge.net/projects/gencodegenerator" (link not opened), which bears on whether the statistical test is reproducible by third parties.
- Panov and Filatov report that the Euplotid code, to which the 2013 paper gives a special role for the "ideogram", "is no more special than any other genetic code" with respect to their signatures.
- Semantic Scholar's count (27) and Crossref's (20) are low for a 2013 paper with wide press coverage; most discussion of the claim that surfaced in searching is in blogs and preprints rather than journals.
